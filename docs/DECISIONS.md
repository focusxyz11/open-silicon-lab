# Open Silicon Lab: decisions

The owner's decisions and the reasons behind them, dated, oldest first. Until Nov 26, 2026 these lived in Claude's private project notes; they are copied here word for word so the project's thinking is public and versioned.

How to use this file:
- Add new decisions at the bottom, dated, with the reason.
- A later decision can reverse an earlier one; say which, and leave the earlier line as it was.
- For where things stand right now, read `docs/STATUS.md` and `docs/ROADMAP.md`; this file is the history and the why.

## Before the Nov 9, 2026 restart (the previous version)

- Building an interactive site to learn the whole chip process (design, manufacturing, AI-era ecosystem) through visualization, simulation and challenges, not a textbook.
- Wants a strong RISC-V / open-source community focus and an AI-native, future-looking perspective.
- Content should never be locked behind information or challenges; everything connected like a puzzle.
- Decided to merge earlier versions into one project named Open Silicon Lab, as a public GitHub repo they created: focusxyz11/open-silicon-lab (GitHub Pages enabled).
- Workflow: discuss ideas in Claude chat, then build through Claude Code on the web.
- Originally a personal self-learning project; now (Nov 2026) aims big for it as a public project.
- Wants (Nov 2026) the chip-building part open and aimed at the big goal, community innovation build rather than only a tiny chip, so it never grows into a separate system from the main thread. Human–AI collaboration in the ecosystem as AI for chip design arrives is a concept to always consider; imagines the whole project as a community-driven, self-organizing system.
- Decided to make the lab universal: about all chips, not only AI chips (affects the intro and overall logic).
- Planned features: build the RISC-V lab's ALU from your own gates, export designs as Verilog toward Tiny Tapeout, a 1971–2040 time slider, and challenges verifying an AI agent's designs.
- Wants to explore next-generation computer architectures in a later round.
- Decided to start next with a topic on "why can a perfect design fail in the real world".
- Decided on a modular architecture, done in small steps: each topic its own folder, loaded only when opened; for speed, growing context, and collaboration with other people who may use less capable AIs.
- Wants the whole site challenge-based and visual-first, designed for the future, guiding people to think better; key structure and concepts should stay stable as new and conflicting ideas keep being added.
- Plans multi-language support in the future: no translation needed soon, but wants the structure designed multi-language-ready now; expects content to grow well beyond 1M.
- Finds the home map's crossing lines too complicated and the Knowledge/Challenges cards boring with no visible connections; wants to choose a better visualization together.
- Wants more big-picture questions like that one, about challenges beyond the technology itself (ecosystem, supply chain, transformation in the AI era), with a future perspective.
- Likes "learners become contributors" and "plug in real hardware" ideas, but keeps both as long-term goals to build step by step, not yet.
- Later decided to start trying "plug in real hardware", keeping it open and future-oriented.
- Feels the first-visit guide is not good because it forces visitors to go through it; wants a better way.
- Decided to prioritize the flagship "build your own RISC-V chip, start to finish" journey next, with added visualization of the whole process, which they think would make the project unique.
- Believes simulating not-yet-existing future computer architectures could drive transformation and innovation; wants that as a future part of the lab.
- Picked next ideas (Nov 2026): a "physics floor" view, a community chip from learners' designs, and "from need to chip" — the last for the future, possibly as a public MCP server.
- Wants brainstormed ideas written down and kept, since some may lead to other ideas or help later.
- Leaning (Nov 2026) toward focusing on concepts (innovation, challenges) and visuals over high-fidelity simulation, worried that adding more real tools and code would turn it into another project; wants the simulation/code parts visualized; doesn't expect anyone to build a real chip there for now, but sees the simulated design-and-build process as what makes it unique; visitors (and they) needn't learn everything.
- Thinks the community project should explore innovation as collective intelligence; building a chip alone isn't enough. Sees collective intelligence as a kind of next-generation computer architecture / new innovation design; wants the site more visual and interesting to attract a community to join.
- Decided (Nov 2026) to focus on reorganizing and strengthening existing content and design before implementing new ideas; aims for world-class design that attracts a community and inspires and motivates people exploring and learning.
- Redesign approach (Nov 2026): make several prototypes of the system and design first, then implement. Found the first template-like prototypes not good enough; chose to combine the "rack-to-atom zoom" and the "die as the map of the lab" directions into one, and wants a light/dark mode switch.
- Wants (Nov 2026) the site transformed from knowledge/information-based to challenge-based, visual-first and future-oriented, with deep connections and one big picture; approved building it step by step, starting with the challenges page as a "frontier map" (build, break, imagine, and open-problem walls).
- Feels (Nov 2026) the site's Beginner/Engineer levels differ too little in content and visuals, and questions whether separate levels are needed; wants complex theories to be understandable to people with zero knowledge or very young students, which they see as fitting the visual-first concept.
- Wants (Nov 2026) the first page redesigned to grab visitors emotionally and avoid information overload; thinks "What changed recently" doesn't belong there. Found the first three first-page prototypes not informational or attractive enough, and had no clear direction yet. Found a cinematic "first five seconds" test too fancy with no real core content: good design matters, but content must excel the looks. Then put the first-page redesign on hold for now.
- Own idea (Nov 2026): a real chip manufacturing line as a 3D simulation, so learners understand the system much better, which could expand to the whole innovation ecosystem. Chose to implement it next, ahead of the other brainstormed ideas.
- Going with (Nov 2026) replacing the Beginner/Engineer levels with one picture explored at growing depth (See it, Measure it, Model it), prototyped first on yield and quantum.
- Felt (Nov 2026) the project structure had become complicated after many brainstorming rounds, and agreed to review and reorganize it. Going with: one Challenges page replacing the Build, Break, Imagine and Frontier doors; the six smaller "new ways to compute" topics kept as pages under one map stop; a new memory topic.

## 26 November 2026, the path and the stack

Recorded in the repository on the day (the first version of this file); kept word for word.

- **The physics-to-intelligence path is the lab's spine.** "Build a thinking machine from physics up, and invent your
  own along the way", in the spirit of Nand to Tetris for the AI era: what you build on one rung is carried up and used
  by the next. Why: one promise anyone can understand, buildable step by step, leading straight to the next
  generation of computer architecture, with the thirteen questions as lenses on it (`docs/PATH.md`).
- **Your stack is the second core visual system, after the dive.** Why: the dive shows how a real chip is; the stack
  shows the one you made, and it is designed to be as strong as the dive (prototype by Claude Fable 5.2,
  `docs/prototypes/stack/`).
- **Seven rungs, shared with the dive and the lattice:** atom, switch, gate, block, chip, machine, mind. Why: one
  ladder seen three ways, so the stack is not a second system; sums and memory are parts of the block, cores part of
  the chip.
- **A recipe of four genes is a set of forks in the stack.** *Grow an architecture* and *Your stack* become one model:
  the stack computes a recipe's anatomy, the architecture engine grows its maturity. Why: the next generation of
  architecture is the path seen from above.
- **Grow an architecture's own race and map of 400 retire into the stack world;** its two challenges, family tree,
  curve and keeping in the ledger stay. **The lattice becomes an arrangement of the same ladder,** not a separate
  picture. Why: the growth bar and the field already do both jobs; nothing shown twice.
- **Diversity by design:** the best per niche, never one leaderboard; an open genome, its public form only after the
  first forks carry; credit for trying, not only for winning; agents explore, people choose. Why: a field that keeps
  one champion collapses back onto today's computer; new ideas need a way in.

  step. The owner had no preference; it stays open until the framework settles.

  styles, loading).
