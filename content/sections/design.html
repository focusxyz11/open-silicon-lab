Design

A chip starts as code, not silicon

Chip designers describe hardware in a hardware description language such as Verilog. Software from electronic design automation (EDA) companies then turns that description into billions of transistors and the wires between them. Nothing physical exists until tape-out, when the finished layout is sent to the factory.

The work moves through a fixed sequence of steps. Pick any step to see what happens there and how AI is now changing it.

A static CMOS full adder needs about 28 transistors. A Rubin GPU has 336 billion transistors, enough for roughly 12 billion of these if it were made of nothing else.

Teams of hundreds to thousands of engineers split into architecture, RTL design, verification, physical design, analog and I/O, software, and post-silicon validation. Most RTL is written in SystemVerilog or VHDL. Some teams use Chisel, a hardware language built on Scala, or high-level synthesis that turns C++ into RTL. Nobody designs everything from scratch: companies license proven blocks such as CPU cores, memory controllers, and PCIe or Ethernet interfaces, then spend their effort on what makes their chip different.

Three engineering budgets fight each other on every chip: performance, power and area (PPA). The next three simulations show the trade-offs designers make every day.

Timing: every signal must arrive before the next tick

A chip runs on a clock. Between two ticks, a signal must leave one flip-flop, pass through a chain of logic gates and settle at the next flip-flop. The slowest chain, called the critical path, sets the top clock speed. Designers cut long paths with pipeline registers. Each stage gets shorter, so the clock can tick faster, at the cost of more flip-flops and more cycles of delay. Click between gates to add or remove a register, or pick a count.

Power: why chips don't simply run faster

Each time a transistor switches it charges a tiny capacitor, so dynamic power is P = α · C · V² · f: activity × capacitance × voltage squared × frequency. A faster clock needs a higher voltage so transistors switch in time, so power climbs much faster than speed. Leakage adds power even when nothing switches. This is why AI chips use many slower units at low voltage, and why your phone lowers its voltage whenever it can.

Verification: hunting the rare bug

Verification engineers list every situation the design must handle as coverage bins: every instruction type, every queue full or empty, every odd timing overlap. Random tests hit common situations quickly but almost never reach rare corners, and that is exactly where bugs hide. Coverage-directed test generation, now increasingly run by AI agents, looks at what is still uncovered and writes tests aimed at it. Run both and compare the curves.
