From sand to switch

Why silicon can be a switch

Chips start as sand. Sand is silicon dioxide, and silicon is a semiconductor: it conducts electricity a little, somewhere between a metal and glass. That "a little" is the whole trick. Add a few atoms of another element and silicon conducts much better; put a voltage nearby and it can switch from insulator to conductor in a trillionth of a second.

Growing a perfect crystal

A transistor only works in a crystal where every atom sits in its place. So chipmakers melt polysilicon purified to eleven nines (99.999999999% pure) in a quartz crucible at about 1,420 °C, dip in a small seed crystal, and pull it slowly upward while turning it. Atoms freeze onto the seed in the same pattern, and a single crystal up to two metres long grows over a day or two. This is the Czochralski process, invented in 1916.

You run the puller. First pull a thin neck to shed defects, then widen the shoulder, then hold the diameter at 300 mm for as long as you can. Pulling faster or running the melt hotter makes the crystal thinner.

Usable wafers–

Teaching model: diameter responds to pull speed and melt temperature with a lag, as in real pullers, which use cameras and closed-loop control. Real 300 mm pulls run at roughly 0.5–1 mm per minute; the clock here is sped up. Wafers are counted at 775 µm thick, the 300 mm standard, plus about 200 µm lost to each wire-saw cut.

From ingot to wafer

The ingot is ground to an exact cylinder, notched to mark its crystal direction, sliced by diamond wire saws, then lapped, etched and polished until its thickness varies by less than a micrometre across 300 mm and its surface is smooth to a fraction of a nanometre. Scaled up to the size of a football field, a finished wafer would have bumps no taller than a grain of sand.

Doping by bombardment: ion implantation

To make n-type and p-type regions exactly where the design wants them, fabs fire dopant ions into the wafer like microscopic bullets. An ion implanter accelerates boron, phosphorus or arsenic atoms to thousands or millions of volts. The energy sets how deep they go; the dose sets how many. The impacts knock silicon atoms out of place, so the wafer is then annealed: heated for a moment to repair the crystal and lock the dopants into it. Hot anneals also let dopants spread, which blurs the sharp profile that tiny transistors need.
