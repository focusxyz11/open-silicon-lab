Lithography

Printing patterns smaller than the light that draws them

Lithography projects a mask pattern onto a wafer coated with light-sensitive resist. The smallest printable feature follows the Rayleigh equation: half-pitch = k₁ · λ / NA. You can shrink the wavelength λ, raise the lens's numerical aperture NA, or push k₁ down with optical tricks. The industry has done all three.

This simulator computes the real diffraction physics. A grating of lines splits light into diffraction orders. The lens only catches orders that land inside its pupil. If only the straight-through order gets in, the pattern is lost. Try shrinking the pitch with conventional light, then switch on off-axis illumination or double patterning.

The machine

EUV light at 13.5 nm is made by hitting tiny droplets of molten tin with a laser tens of thousands of times per second. Only ASML builds EUV scanners. Its High-NA EUV system raises NA from 0.33 to 0.55 for 8 nm resolution and costs around $400 million. In July 2026 Intel became the first to ship high-volume logic chips (some Panther Lake processors on Intel 18A) with select layers printed by High-NA. TSMC owns High-NA tools but still considers them too costly for mass production.

Inside the EUV scanner: an energy budget

EUV light is absorbed by almost everything, including air and glass, so the whole light path sits in vacuum and uses mirrors instead of lenses. Each mirror is a stack of roughly 40 pairs of molybdenum and silicon layers, and even so it reflects only about 70% of the light. After a dozen reflections, only a few percent of the source power reaches the wafer. That budget decides how many wafers per hour the machine can print. Change the numbers and watch the throughput.

ASML's NXE:3800E prints about 230 wafers per hour with a source near 600 W. In February 2026 ASML reported a path to 1,000 W by firing at about 100,000 tin droplets per second, aiming for roughly 330 wafers per hour by 2030.

Why dose matters: counting photons

An EUV photon carries 92 eV, about 14 times more energy than a 193 nm photon, so the same dose delivers 14 times fewer photons. At tiny sizes the random count of photons in each spot makes edges rough and can leave a contact hole missing or merged. Lower the dose to print faster and watch the holes degrade.

Model: 9 contact holes 16 nm wide on a 32 nm pitch, 1 nm pixels. Photons arrive randomly (Poisson statistics), about 20% are absorbed, chemical blur spreads them over about 3 nm, then a threshold develops the resist. A hole fails if its printed area is below 60% or above 150% of target. Real EUV resists add secondary electrons and chemistry that this skips.

Throughput model: exposure time = dose × 700 cm² ÷ power at the wafer, plus 12.6 s per wafer for stage moves, alignment and wafer exchange. Calibrated to about 230 wafers per hour at 600 W and 50 mJ/cm².
