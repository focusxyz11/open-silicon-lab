The physics floor

How far are chips from the limits of physics?

Engineers keep making chips faster and more efficient, but physics sets floors that no design can get under. The deepest one: erasing one bit of information costs a minimum amount of energy, set only by temperature. Rolf Landauer showed it in 1961, and experiments have since measured it. Ordinary logic erases bits all the time, so every calculation has a floor.

Energies are measured values for a 45 nm chip from about 2012 (Dally and colleagues, the same table as in Beyond today's computers): reading 16 bits costs 0.12 pJ from registers, 11 pJ from on-chip memory and 640 pJ from DRAM. Today's best chips do arithmetic several times more cheaply and move bits somewhat more cheaply, so every bar sits lower, but the gaps stay this large. The floor counts about b × b + 2b bits erased per b-bit multiply-add, an estimate of the order of magnitude. A language model needs about one multiply-add per parameter for each word it writes; a ResNet-50-sized image network needs about 4 billion (He and colleagues, 2015).

Why not just make the chip colder?

The floor falls with temperature: at 4 kelvin, a bit costs 75 times less to erase. But the heat still has to leave, and pumping heat from cold to warm costs work. Even a perfect refrigerator pays exactly as much as you saved.

The room is 300 K. An ideal (Carnot) refrigerator moving heat Q from temperature T up to the room needs work Q × (300 − T) / T; real ones manage only a fraction of that. Cold chips are still useful: quantum computers and some superconducting logic need the cold to work at all, not to save energy. The only way under the floor is to stop erasing bits: see Reversible computing.

The speed floor

No signal travels faster than light. Inside a chip, signals run through glass-like insulation, where light itself is about half as fast as in vacuum, and real wires are slower still. So the faster the clock, the less of the chip a signal can reach in one tick.

Light in silicon dioxide travels at c / √3.9, about half its speed in vacuum. Real on-chip wires are much slower than this, because each wire must charge its own capacitance through its resistance, so this is a floor, not what chips achieve. The largest chip is the diagonal of the 26 × 33 mm area a lithography scanner prints in one exposure.
