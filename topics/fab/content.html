Inside the fab

Manufacturing at the edge of physics

A fab repeats one loop over and over: put down a film, pattern it with lithography, etch, fill, polish, inspect. Transistors are built first at the bottom, then a dozen or more layers of wiring are stacked above them. Leading-edge chips need 70 or more mask layers and well over a thousand individual steps, and every step must work on nearly every one of billions of features. A process that is 99% good per step would leave almost nothing after a thousand steps, which is why fabs chase perfection.

Equipment lab 1 · The cleanroom

How clean is clean?

A single dust particle landing on a wafer can short two wires or block a pattern. Fabs filter air so it flows down in smooth sheets, keep people in full-body suits, and carry wafers between tools in sealed pods (FOUPs) on overhead robot tracks. Equipment lab 2 · Deposition

Laying down films one atomic layer at a time

Modern transistors need films only a few atoms thick that coat deep, narrow trenches evenly. Ordinary chemical vapour deposition (CVD) is fast but piles material up at the trench opening, which can seal it and trap a void. Atomic layer deposition (ALD) alternates two chemicals that each react only with the exposed surface and then stop. Each cycle adds about 0.1 nm everywhere, at the top and the bottom alike. It is slow, but nearly perfect.

Equipment lab 3 · Plasma etch

Carving straight down

In a plasma etcher, an electric field accelerates ions straight down onto the wafer, while neutral radicals drift in every direction and react chemically. Ions give straight walls. Chemistry gives speed and selectivity, but eats sideways under the mask. Etching a hole 50 or more times deeper than it is wide, as 3D NAND memory needs, is one of the hardest jobs in the fab.

Equipment lab 4 · Overlay

Lining up layer after layer

Each of the 70+ layers is printed separately, and every via must land on the wire beneath it. The scanner measures alignment marks and corrects for the wafer stretching and heating, yet small random errors remain. With billions of vias per chip, even a one-in-a-million miss rate means a dead chip.

Wires and vias are 14 nm wide on a 28 nm pitch, and a chip has 10 billion vias. A via fails here if less than 70% of it lands on its wire. Fabs also use self-aligned vias that snap to the wire below, and add design margins, which relax this budget.

Equipment lab 5 · Inspection

Finding one defect among billions of features

Inspection tools scan wafers after critical steps. Because every die is identical, the classic trick is die-to-die comparison: image three neighbouring dies and flag any spot where one disagrees with the other two. Machine-learning models then sort real killer defects from harmless noise. Try to spot the defect yourself first.

Optical and electron-beam inspection tools come from KLA, Applied Materials and Hitachi High-Tech. In 2026 TSMC began adopting NVIDIA vision-AI tools to find rare defects with less manual labelling.
