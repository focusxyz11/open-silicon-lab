Chips that touch the real world

How does a chip feel the world?

A processor only understands numbers. Everything it knows about the world, a heartbeat, a voice, the temperature of a room, arrives through chips that work with smooth, continuous voltages: analog chips. A sensor turns the world into a tiny voltage, an amplifier makes it bigger, and an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) measures it many times a second and writes down each measurement as a number with a fixed number of bits.

Every choice costs energy. More bits and more measurements make a truer copy, but each number also has to be stored or sent by radio. Try to catch each signal well enough while staying inside its battery budget.

Aliasing: when too few samples tell a lie

To capture a wave you need more than two samples per cycle of its fastest part. Sample slower and a fast vibration shows up as a slow one that is not really there. In the motor mission, a faint 1.2 kHz tone is the bearing starting to fail: sample too slowly and it vanishes from the copy.

Power: the chip that feeds all the others

Every chip needs a steady voltage, usually lower than the battery's. A linear regulator (LDO) drops the extra voltage across a transistor and turns it into heat: simple and quiet, but wasteful when the drop is large. A switching regulator (buck) chops the voltage on and off through a coil: far more efficient, but it leaves a small ripple on the output, which sensitive circuits can hear. Pick the right one for each job.

A teaching model with round values: the LDO's 0.15 V dropout and tiny quiescent current; the buck's fixed overhead (60 µW), conduction and switching losses, and 15 mV of ripple. Real regulators vary widely, and many switch to a low-power mode at light loads. When two choices are about equally efficient, the simpler one (fewer parts, no coil) is the better design.

Why analog chips stay on older factories

Digital logic gets cheaper and faster with every smaller process. Analog circuits mostly do not: precise amplifiers and converters need large, well-matched transistors, and power chips handle voltages that tiny transistors cannot survive. So sensors, converters and power chips are usually made on mature processes, which is why a shortage of cheap, old chips can stop a car factory.
