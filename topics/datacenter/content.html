Memory, networks & data centers

Feeding the beast

A modern AI chip can do more math than its memory can feed, more than its network can share, and more than many power grids can supply. So in 2026 the race is as much about memory cells, cables and megawatts as about transistors. This stage follows a number from a memory cell, through a memory stack and a network of thousands of chips, to the power plant.

Stacking memory: the HBM builder

HBM stacks DRAM dies on top of a logic base die, joined by thousands of vertical copper wires called through-silicon vias. The stack must still fit a fixed package height, so every extra layer means grinding each die thinner. Build a stack and see where it breaks.

Wiring thousands of chips together

Big models are split across many chips, which must swap results constantly. Inside a scale-up domain, such as NVIDIA's 72-GPU rack, chips talk over short copper NVLink cables at 3.6 TB/s each, so the rack acts like one giant GPU. Between racks, scale-out networks of Ethernet or InfiniBand switches carry about 0.2 TB/s per GPU, over optical fibre because copper can't reach. Every optical link needs a transceiver at each end, and at data-center scale those lasers add up to megawatts. Co-packaged optics, which reached production switches in 2026, move the optics right next to the switch chip to cut that power.

Teaching model: a non-blocking fat-tree built from 102.4 Tb/s switches with 64 ports of 1.6 Tb/s, one 1.6 Tb/s network port per GPU, and an optical transceiver at both ends of every switch link. Vendor claims put a pluggable 1.6 Tb/s module near 30 W and a co-packaged port near 9 W; the GPU-side module stays pluggable. Switch systems are counted at 1.5 kW each. Real clusters vary widely.

From chip to power plant

Data centers used about 485 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2025, roughly as much as a large European country, and the International Energy Agency expects that to nearly double to about 950 TWh by 2030, with AI-focused sites tripling. Every token an AI model writes costs a little energy at the chip, more in the server around it, and more again for cooling and power conversion. Change the assumptions and follow one watt.

Tokens per second depend heavily on the model size, number format and how many users share each chip: a single chat user sees 50–200 tokens per second, while a busy server can produce thousands per chip. PUE (power usage effectiveness) is total facility power divided by IT power; modern liquid-cooled sites reach about 1.1–1.2. The 1 GW campus assumes full load all year.
