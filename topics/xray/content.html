Chip X-ray

What can you read from the inside of a chip?

Take the lid off a chip and look under a microscope, and you see a die: a flat piece of silicon tiled with blocks. You cannot see single transistors any more, but every kind of block has its own texture. Memory is a perfectly regular grid, because one tiny cell is copied millions of times. Logic looks like fine noise, because software placed millions of different gates. Processor cores appear as identical copies of one tile.

Learn the clues, then read a chip like an engineer: where the area goes tells you what a chip was built to do.

A teaching model: each floorplan is a typical arrangement drawn in general terms from public die photos and floorplans, not one product, and different chips are not drawn to the same scale. "Other" is wiring, spacing and test structures between the blocks, and the ring of input and output circuits at the edge, behind the pads. The textures are drawn to show each block's clue, not copied from a photo.

The area tells the story

Designers pay for every square millimetre, so the floorplan shows their priorities. A microcontroller spends most of its area on memory and on circuits that talk to the outside world; its CPU is a speck. An AI GPU is a sea of identical cores with memory interfaces down both sides. A memory chip is almost nothing but memory. Try it without labels.
