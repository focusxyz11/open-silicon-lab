Compute in memory

Let the memory do the multiplying

A neural network spends most of its energy reading weights from memory. So why not compute where the weights are? Store each weight as the conductance of a memory cell in a grid. Apply the inputs as voltages on the rows. By Ohm's law each cell passes a current equal to voltage times conductance, and by Kirchhoff's law the currents on each column add up. One step gives a whole matrix-vector product, with no data moved at all. The catch: the answer is analog, so it is only as good as the devices and the converter that reads it.

An 8 × 4 crossbar; each weight is a pair of cells so it can be negative. Device variation adds random error to every cell; the ADC rounds each column current. Energy is an illustrative model: digital pays an SRAM read and a multiply-add per weight; analog pays almost nothing in the array but a converter per column whose cost roughly doubles with every extra bit. Real chips also fight drift, temperature and wire resistance.

Try this: find the fewest ADC bits that keep the error under 5%. Then raise device variation and see how fast that answer changes. This is the daily trade-off for compute-in-memory designers, and why the first products target small edge AI models that tolerate a little error.
