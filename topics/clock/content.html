Memory and the clock

How does a chip remember, and why does it need a clock?

The gates you have built so far forget: their output follows their inputs, and the moment the inputs change, the old answer is gone. A processor has to hold numbers between steps. The trick is to wire gates in a loop, so each one keeps the other's answer alive. That loop is a bit of memory.

Every signal here moves in slow motion: one second on screen is less than a billionth of a second in the chip. Each gate takes a few tens of picoseconds (ps, trillionths of a second) to react.

The clock: change only on the tick

A loop that remembers is not enough. A processor has thousands of them, and each must take its new value at the same moment, after the answers feeding it have settled. So chips have a clock: a signal that ticks between 0 and 1 billions of times a second. A latch lets its input through the whole time the clock is 1. A flip-flop, two latches back to back, takes its input only at the instant the clock rises.

A counter, and the speed limit

Put four flip-flops in a row and feed their outputs through a chain of half adders (the first circuit in the tiny-chip studio) back into their inputs. On every tick the register stores its old value plus one: a counter. Between ticks, the new value has to get out of the flip-flops, ripple through the adders and settle in time for the next tick. If the clock ticks sooner than that, the register stores a half-finished answer.

The counter is simulated gate by gate with the studio's cell delays at each process. The top safe speed adds up the slowest path: clock to flip-flop output, the carry chain through the adders, and the setup time. Simplified: no wire delays, no clock skew (the tick arriving at slightly different times), no voltage or temperature changes. Real processors stopped getting faster clocks around 2005 at about 4 GHz, even though their gates kept getting quicker, because power and heat, not gate delay, became the limit.
