Supply chain map

One chip, a dozen countries

A single AI accelerator is designed in California with British instruction sets and American software, printed in Taiwan on Japanese wafers by Dutch, American and Japanese machines, stacked with Korean memory, packaged in Taiwan, built into servers in Taiwan or Mexico, and switched on in a data center in Texas. Each country owns the steps where it built decades of skill, so no country can make an advanced chip alone.

Who owns each step

Each row is one activity, and its cells show how the world's value added in that activity splits by region. Darker cells mean a larger share. Design and software are led by American companies; equipment by the US, Japan and the Netherlands; manufacturing and assembly happen mostly in East Asia. The percentage after each activity is its share of the whole industry's value.

What if one link breaks?

Because each step is concentrated, a single earthquake, fire, blockade or export ban can stop products all over the world. Choose a disruption and see how supply of five product types might respond over the next two years. Inventory buys time; second sources take months to qualify; and some links, such as EUV scanners, have no second source at all.

Spreading the risk: the new fabs

Governments now pay chipmakers to build closer to home: the US CHIPS Act, the EU Chips Act, and subsidies in Japan, Korea, India and China. The share of the most advanced capacity outside Taiwan is rising, but slowly, because a leading fab takes years to build and even longer to reach full yield.
