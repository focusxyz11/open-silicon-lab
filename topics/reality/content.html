Why perfect designs fail

Why can a perfect design fail in the real world?

Before a chip is made, engineers check it in simulation: is the logic right, does every signal arrive on time? That check uses one ideal chip: a typical one, at 25 °C, on a steady supply, brand new. No real chip is like that. Each one comes out a little different. They run hot, the supply dips under load, transistors age, the factory test misses a few broken ones, and radiation flips bits.

Below, a design that passed every check goes out into the world. Pick where it will live, see what breaks, and choose fixes. Every fix costs speed, power, area or money, which is why a car chip is not built like a phone chip.

How the model works: chip-to-chip spread is a normal distribution (σ = 3% of delay); delay follows the alpha-power law of transistors; heat slows them by 0.08% per °C; the supply dips 5% under load (2% with a stronger grid); aging raises the threshold voltage with the fifth root of time, faster when hot and at higher voltage; wires wear out on a lognormal curve (Black's equation); hidden weak spots follow a Weibull curve; radiation causes about 500 bit upsets per billion hours per megabit of memory at sea level and 300 times more in low orbit, and 30% of them matter. Real chips also crack from heat cycles, absorb total radiation dose and fail in other ways left out here. All numbers are teaching values, not any company's data.
